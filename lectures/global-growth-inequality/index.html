part one

Growth

part one  ·  growth

THE WORLD IS GROWING

growth  ·  the 90s–10s

The 90s–10s

A period of unprecedented growth in the developing world.
A period of industrialization and development in some regions.
A period of stagnation in others.

growth  ·  over time

Growth has increased over time

The industrial revolution led to 0.5% annual productivity growth.
The prewar era led to 1% annual productivity growth.
The postwar era had 3% annual productivity growth.
Postwar Asia had 7–8%.

growth  ·  convergence

Convergence is uncommon

Developing late should mean faster development.
But most developing countries do not grow more quickly than developed economies.
There are meaningful exceptions (East Asia).
Development scholars have identified a few things correlated with faster growth:
human capital
investment
institution quality
exposure to trade
stability
But all of those things have serious endogeneity problems.

growth  ·  development

Economic development goes hand-in-hand with diversification

Poor countries have structurally different economies than rich countries.
To industrialize, people have to move and radically transform their lifestyles in disruptive ways.

growth  ·  industries

Not all industries are equally valuable

Virtually all sustained high growth rates came from manufacturing booms.
Only oil can compete with manufacturing in terms of growth potential.
Manufacturing sectors experience convergence faster than other industries.
Even successful manufacturing sectors make up small portions of their economies.

part two  ·  inequality

THE WORLD IS UNEQUAL

inequality  ·  three types

Three Types of Inequality

Inequality 1: International Inequality
Inequality 2: Population Inequality
Inequality 3: Global Inequality

inequality 1

International Inequality

How wealthy a nation is compared to other nations.
Often measured by GDP or mean/median household income.
Rich countries and poor countries.

inequality 2

Population Inequality

How wealthy a nation is, considering their population.
Often measured by GDP per capita.
Very large poor countries matter more for inequality.

inequality 3

Global Inequality

Drop countries and states entirely.
A poor person in a middle-income country might be a wealthy person in a poor country.
Hard to collect data on.

the implication

Which type of inequality we look at matters

inequality  ·  winners

Who Won Globalization?

The top 1% of global income had a 60% increase from 2000–2010.
The global median had an 80% increase during the same period.
The bottom 1/3 had 40–70% income increases.
The number of people in “absolute poverty” fell in half during those years.

inequality  ·  the rest

Who Lost Did Not Win Globalization?

The poorest 5% saw no income change.
The global upper-middle class (75th–90th percentile) saw no income change.

inequality  ·  the global elite

Who are the Global Elite — top 1%?

60 million people.
12% of Americans.
3–6% of Britons, Japanese, Germans, and French.
1% of Italians, Spaniards, Portuguese, Greeks, Brazilians, Russians, and South Africans.

inequality  ·  asia

Consider Asia

In 1988, the median income Chinese person was richer than 10% of the world’s population.
In 2008, the median income Chinese person was richer than 50% of the world’s population.
In 1988, the median income Indian person was richer than 10% of the world’s population.
In 2008, the median income Indian person was richer than 27% of the world’s population.

inequality  ·  losers

Losers in that period:

Many African countries.
Some Latin American countries.
Most post-communist countries.

inequality  ·  examples

Examples:

The average Kenyan went from the 22nd to the 12th percentile.
The average Nigerian went from the 16th to the 13th percentile.
despite overall growth

inequality  ·  sources

Sources of Inequality:

Two sources:
differences within nations
differences between nations
In 1870, difference within nations accounted for 2/3 of global inequality.
In 2010, differences between nations accounted for 2/3 of global inequality.
Location has replaced Class.
Where you are born matters more than whether or not you are rich or poor where you are.

inequality  ·  modern wealth

Modern Wealth:

Citizenship / residence is the greatest determinant of standard of living.
Inequality causes tension. Tension causes conflict.
Only three ways to reduce the tension:
poor countries achieve high growth rates
global redistribution is implemented by policy
the people in the poor countries come to the rich countries

part three

Immigration

immigration  ·  what you study

Immigration Policy: Depends on what you study

Prejudice and Racism? Immigration policy is just a form of oppression.
Political Economy of Income? Immigration policy is a fight between labor and capital.
State and Institutional Concerns? Immigration is about fiscal costs of immigrants vs economic benefit of labor.

immigration  ·  the trade tradeoff

The Trade Tradeoff

Trade policy affects the composition of firms.
Trade policy changes what inputs are needed.
Immigration policy changes the availability of labor.
Immigration and Free Trade are in TENSION in developed economies.

immigration  ·  trade posture

Trade posture is not exogenous

Policymakers choose a trade policy that is affected by lobbyists or bribes.
Closed trade harms consumers and helps domestic industry.
Open trade harms domestic industry and helps consumers.
Domestic industries lobby for protection, and then require labor.

immigration  ·  causes

Complicated Causes

Does trade policy cause immigration policy?
Or does immigration policy cause trade policy?
Might open immigration remove specialization?
Might trade remove regional inequality?

Late Postwar Era

Trade continues to expand.
Immigration only expands slightly.
